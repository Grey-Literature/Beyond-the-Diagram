Lab: Packet captures with AI help

There's a mechanical problem here before there's an interesting one, and it stops most people on the first move.

The mechanical problem: it's a binary file

A .pcap / .pcapng is binary. Most AI tools can't parse one directly, and the ones that claim to are generally converting it behind the scenes anyway. You have to extract a text slice first — which means you need enough filter syntax to narrow the extract to the conversation you actually care about: 

tshark -r capture.pcapng \
  -Y "bootp || dns" \
  -T fields -e frame.time -e ip.src -e ip.dst -e _ws.col.Info

That -Y display filter is the whole ballgame. A 400 MB capture off a busy VLAN is not something you hand to anything, human or otherwise — you narrow it to a protocol, a host pair, and a time window first.

Notice what that filtering step actually is: half-splitting, one layer earlier than you'd normally apply it. You're reducing the haystack before anything looks at it, not asking something else to search the whole haystack for you. Same discipline, different target.

The real problem: an anomaly needs a baseline

Here's the part worth naming directly, because it's the part that doesn't get taught. An AI staring at a reduced capture still can't tell you what's wrong with it — and neither can a person — without knowing what was supposed to happen. Anomaly detection is a comparison. If you don't supply the other side of it, there's nothing to compare against, and what comes back is either a generic protocol tutorial or a confident invention.

Call this baseline articulation: being able to state, before you look, which endpoint should be talking to which, over what protocol, in what order, and roughly how often.

No baseline "Here's a packet capture from a machine that can't get an IP. What's wrong?"

Best case, you get a description of what DHCP is. Worst case, you get a confident identification of an "anomaly" that's completely normal traffic, because nothing in the prompt let it distinguish normal from abnormal.
Baseline stated "This client on VLAN 20 should broadcast a DHCPDISCOVER, and our relay should forward it to the DHCP server at 10.0.4.5, which should return a DHCPOFFER within a couple of retries. Here's a filtered extract of all bootp traffic from this client's MAC over 90 seconds. Tell me which of those steps you can and can't see, and don't fill in anything you don't actually observe in the data."

That second prompt does three things the first doesn't: it states the expected sequence, it scopes the evidence, and it explicitly forbids filling gaps with plausible invention. The answer it gets back is checkable — you can look at the extract and confirm or refute every claim in it.

Where the baseline actually comes from

Directly from the domain modules. You cannot write "should get a DHCPOFFER back from 10.0.4.5 within a couple of retries" without already understanding the DHCP lease cycle and knowing your own relay topology. You cannot state the expected DNS resolution chain without knowing what the chain is.

So this sub-skill isn't really a new AI trick. It's the AI-fluency module and the protocol-mechanism modules meeting in the middle: you supply the expected topology and protocol context, the AI helps comb the reduced extract for violations of it. Take away your half and it has nothing to work with. That's not a limitation to work around — it's the correct division of labor.

What to include when you hand over an extract

  • The expected sequence, step by step, in the order it should occur.
  • Named endpoints and their roles — which IP is the client, the relay, the server, the gateway.
  • The capture point. A capture taken at the client sees a completely different world than one taken at the server or on a SPAN port mid-path — and "no DHCPOFFER visible" means something entirely different depending on where you were standing.
  • The time window and the filter you applied, so absence can be interpreted. If you filtered to bootp, an absence of DNS traffic proves nothing.
  • An instruction not to infer beyond the data. "Only tell me what you can actually see in these lines" is worth typing every time.
That fourth point is Signal vs. Silence showing up again, in a form that's easy to miss: in a filtered capture, silence is ambiguous by construction — you can't tell "it didn't happen" from "I filtered it out" unless you say what you filtered.

Try it

Take a capture of something working normally — not something broken. A single machine renewing a DHCP lease, or resolving a name, is plenty.

  1. Write the baseline first, before you look at the capture. Which endpoints, which protocol, what order, roughly what timing.
  2. Filter and export a text slice with tshark, scoped to that conversation.
  3. Hand both to an AI and ask it which steps of your stated baseline it can and can't observe.
  4. Then check its answer against the extract yourself, line by line.

Doing this on known-good traffic is the point: you already know the answer, so you can catch it inventing. Get comfortable spotting that on traffic you understand before you rely on it for traffic you don't.
