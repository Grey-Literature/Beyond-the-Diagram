Diligence

Lab: Diligence and the agentic boundary

"The AI did it" is not an excuse. You allowed it. You instructed it. You deployed the output. A tool doesn't carry accountability — your name is on it.

This is the leg with actual professional consequences attached, and it splits into what you feed in, what you disclose, and what you vouch for on the way out.

Creation Diligence — what goes in is a decision

  • Credentials never. Pre-shared keys, passwords, API tokens, private keys. There is no prompt worth it and no session so urgent it changes.
  • Environment detail is a judgment call, not a default. Topology, hostnames, IP schemes, who your client is and what they run. Sometimes necessary to get a useful answer; always a decision you should be making consciously rather than pasting past.
  • Where the model runs is part of evaluating it. Whose infrastructure, under what jurisdiction, with what retention. That's a platform-selection criterion alongside capability, not a footnote after it.
  • A hosted connector is one more trust boundary. Every integration that reaches into your systems is a thing that has access. Prefer running it yourself where you can.

Transparency Diligence — the level depends on audience and stakes

  • Internal, formatting help: low ceremony. Implied, not announced.
  • Client-facing incident summary: the content must be accurate and defensible regardless of how it was drafted. The bar is the content, not the disclosure.
  • Published or shared work: acknowledge the collaboration.
  • AI tooling in a client engagement: relationship-level awareness is the minimum bar. Not every prompt — but they should know it's in the workflow.

Perception shouldn't drive this. Some people will read disclosure as a red flag and some as professionalism, and neither reaction changes what the right level was. The stakes and the relationship decide it.

Deployment Diligence — you vouch for it

Before it goes anywhere with your name on it: is every claim in here something you can point to evidence for? The failure mode isn't a typo. It's output that goes out describing work you didn't do, claiming verification you didn't perform, asserting conclusions you didn't reach — and it goes out looking better than what actually happened, which is exactly why it survives a quick read. That's the Case in this module.

The three modes, and why the distinction is operational

Interactions come in three shapes. The difference isn't how conversational it felt — it's who acted.

Worth stating where the volume actually sits: ITOps work is overwhelmingly Augmentation, on the order of 90–95% of it — research, discovery, remediation and documentation, with a human executing every change. The other two modes are a small share of the sessions and most of the exposure, which is the entire reason this distinction is worth being pedantic about.

ModeWhat happensBlast radius
Automation It executes a task you specified, the way you specified it Whatever you pointed it at, on purpose
Augmentation It advises, analyzes, drafts. You review, decide, and execute Contained to your own workflow — the model touches your context, not the system
Agency It acts on a system directly. The action is taken, not suggested The system. And the change is already in
A wrong firewall rule pushed by an agent doesn't get walked back with "well, the AI suggested it." The rule is in. The outage is real. Augmentation and Agency can feel identical from the keyboard — same chat window, same amount of typing — and they are not the same category of risk. Classifying which one you're in, before you act, is its own drill.

Disclosure follows blast radius

  • Your own systems. Your call, your risk, your lesson.
  • Client systems you manage, AI advising you. Relationship-level awareness that AI tooling is part of how you work.
  • AI touching client systems directly. Explicit consent before the session starts — not disclosed afterward.

The personal-account boundary

Running agentic AI against client systems through your own personal subscription is a liability, and it's one the organization should be carrying rather than an individual absorbing quietly.

  • Read-only missions — pulling logs, reading configs, analyzing output — are manageable on personal tooling.
  • Write, push, execute, deploy requires the professional accountability layer to be explicitly in place, funded, and disclosed by the organization.

An employer doesn't get the benefit of an individual's personal AI investment while hedging on paying for the accountability that comes with it. If that loop isn't closed where you work, read-only is the honest boundary until it is.

Try it

Take the last three things you shipped with AI assistance and answer for each:

  1. Which mode was it? Automation, Augmentation, or Agency. Be strict — if the state of a system changed and no human executed the change, that's Agency regardless of how much you talked about it first.
  2. What went in that shouldn't have? Not a guilt exercise. Check whether environment detail went in because it was needed or because it was in the buffer.
  3. Could you defend every claim in the output? Line by line, against evidence you could actually produce if someone asked six months from now.

Then pick the one with the largest blast radius and ask whether the disclosure level actually matched it — before you shipped, not in hindsight.

Where to practice this safely

Every habit on this page is cheaper to build somewhere mistakes don't cost anyone anything. Breaking your own network carries a completely different risk profile than breaking a client's production environment, and that gap is worth using deliberately rather than working around. A home lab is where you find out what an agent does when it's wrong, before you find out on somebody's live infrastructure.
