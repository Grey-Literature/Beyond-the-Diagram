Delegation

Lab: Deciding whether to involve an AI at all

Delegation happens before prompting, and it's the leg that looks like it isn't there. It gets settled in about the time it takes to pick left or right at a junction you've driven a hundred times — which is not the same as not deciding. The decision ran. It just didn't announce itself.

That distinction matters, because "I don't really make that call, I just open a chat" is almost never true. You picked a tool. You picked which parts of the problem to hand over. You had a rough sense of what you'd accept back. All of it happened below the level where it felt like deliberation — and all of it is still a decision that can be examined, and occasionally is worth examining, when the stakes or the unfamiliarity justify slowing it down.

Problem Awareness — you usually don't have it, and that's the job

In IT the full problem generally isn't understood at the point an assistant gets involved. That's not a failure state; it's the reason a tech was called at all. Sometimes you know how to go find the problem definition. Sometimes you don't, and finding out how is the first real task.

The useful frame here isn't a checklist, it's one you already know. Troubleshooting is the scientific method with worse lighting, and mapping the two makes it obvious where an assistant helps and where it can't.

  1. Ask a question

    In a lab this is the hard, creative part. In ITOps it's handed to you free: the end user already asked it, in the form of a complaint. "Email is slow." "I can't get to the share." That's your question. What you don't get free is any assurance it's the right question — a complaint describes a symptom, and the symptom is not necessarily the problem.

  2. Research

    Where an assistant does most of its actual work in ITOps, and where the choice between push, pull and discovery prompts lives. The signal worth watching: a push prompt that gets no traction is telling you about your prompt, not about the model. It means there wasn't enough context in it. Re-rolling the same push prompt with different wording won't fix that; switching modes will.

  3. Form a hypothesis

    When a model hands you five plausible causes, those aren't answers — they're hypotheses, and a list of them is a legitimate deliverable. A good session goes one step further: it picks a favourite, says why, and says how you'd prove it. A list with no way to test any item on it is a search result with better grammar.

  4. Run an experiment

    "How would I check that" is the experiment — the same pairing the pull-prompt demands of every question it asks you. A hypothesis you can't test isn't one yet, it's a guess wearing better clothes.

  5. Analyze the data

    What actually came back, read verbatim rather than summarized. This is the step where you feed the result into the session so the next round narrows from evidence instead of from your increasingly stale opening description.

  6. Conclusion

    Did the data support the hypothesis? Note that "the fix worked" is not automatically a yes — see symptom vs. root cause. And a disproven hypothesis is a successful experiment: it eliminated a branch, which is the entire point of half-splitting.

It's a loop, not a line. A failed conclusion sends you back to step 3 if you still have hypotheses, and back to step 2 if you're out of them. The common mistake is treating step 3 as the starting line — arriving with a hypothesis already chosen, then using the assistant to build a case for it rather than to test it. That's not research, it's advocacy, and a cooperative model will happily help you do it.

This is also the shape every Case on this platform puts you in on purpose. The hard part of real troubleshooting is almost never executing a known fix — it's steps 1 through 3, before anyone knows what the problem actually is.

Platform Awareness — which one, and at what tier

If you keep more than one model on deck, this is where the real decision sits, and it's the part that runs fastest. Most of the time it resolves on familiarity and proven consistency: you know how this one fails, you know what its output looks like when it's drifting, you know what it does with a vague prompt at 4pm on a Friday. That accumulated calibration is a legitimate criterion, not laziness — it's worth more in practice than a benchmark score, because it's the thing that lets you catch a bad answer quickly.

Things that should override familiarity, and should do it consciously:

  • The shape and size of the data. A three-thousand-row export is a different job from a two-paragraph error message, and the dataset alone is enough to push the choice up a tier regardless of which model you're comfortable in.
  • Whether the thread has to survive across sessions. A multi-stage engagement where research informs design informs documentation needs something that can still hold the plot at step four. A one-shot ticket update does not. Context-dependency picks the tool more often than the subject matter does.
  • How many exchanges you expect. Worth having a personal threshold — "past about four turns this goes to the one I trust with long threads." Crude, and built from watching where things actually degrade, which is the only place that number can come from.
  • Whether it needs current information. Recency and retrieval are a different capability from reasoning. Assuming one tool is good at both is how you get confidently outdated answers.
  • Whether you should be feeding it documentation instead of trusting recall. A model doesn't need to have been trained on your appliance vendor's guide if you hand it the guide. Treated as a reasoning layer over data you supply rather than a recall engine, the knowledge cutoff mostly stops mattering.
  • Whether it's the right tool at all. The most-missed one. If your monitoring already has the answer, going to an assistant is a slower way of not reading your monitoring.
You do not need the largest model available for most of this. If you're deliberate — a real description, evidence fed back each round, hypotheses tested instead of assumed — a mid-range daily-driver model covers the overwhelming majority of ITOps work. Discipline substitutes for horsepower further than people expect, and reaching for the top tier by reflex mostly buys you a slower version of the same answer. Escalate for volume or for genuinely hard reasoning; not as a default.

Two practical notes that follow from that. Most platforms now expose reasoning or thinking-depth toggles, and on some models turning that up is the difference between an assistant that stays on target and one that wanders — it's part of the decision surface, not a setting to leave alone. And the tier you actually want isn't always offered in the interface you happen to be in: the same provider can surface different models in different modes of its own product. Both are worth finding out by experiment rather than assuming the default in front of you is the whole menu.

That last point has a non-AI twin worth recognizing, because the behavior is identical: the tech who keeps asking people about a machine's problem while the uptime graph sitting in front of them says 90 days. Collecting more opinions is not the same as gathering evidence. An assistant is just one more source of opinion if you reach for it in that mode. See Monitoring for the evidence that was already there.
Specimen — a delegation instinct that was wrong

Mid-project, a problem came up inside a large vendor's own ecosystem, so the obvious move was to reach for that vendor's own assistant. Home-field advantage.

There wasn't any. The assistant looped, and rather than admitting it was stuck, it fabricated an error in the input — pointing at a typo that wasn't there — to justify the loop. A plain web search resolved it, because the actual problem was a licensing ambiguity, not a reasoning problem.

The lesson isn't "that assistant is bad." It's that model selection has to match the problem type, not the vendor logo. Home-field advantage is a hypothesis like any other, and it needs the same treatment: cheap to test, not to be assumed.

Task Delegation — the line that doesn't move

Everything is negotiable depending on the task, the tool, and how much brain you have left at that hour — except these:

  • The final call. Somebody decides. It isn't the model.
  • The blast-radius assessment. What breaks if this is wrong, and who notices.
  • Environment-specific context. The thing about this site, this client, this box that isn't written down anywhere and won't be inferred.
  • Professional accountability. Your name is on what ships. Covered properly in Diligence.

Your tool stack is infrastructure — treat it that way

This is the part that gets skipped everywhere, and it's the most ITOps-shaped idea in the whole module: dependency on a single assistant is a single point of failure. Provider has a bad day, your workflow degrades. And if you haven't touched your backup option in three months, the muscle memory for working with it is gone — you have an untested backup, which is to say you have none.

TierWhat it's for
PrimaryProject-class work, complex reasoning, threads that must stay coherent across sessions
SecondaryQuick dispatch, current information, one-shot formatting, offloading mental overhead
TertiaryCreative and lateral work. Know what you're getting — this tier tends to wildcard under pressure
EmergencyWarm enough to actually use on a bad day. Both primary and secondary being degraded at once is a real scenario, not a theoretical one

Keeping it warm is active work, not a note in a document. Periodically ask what's new and worth trying; test new options before you need them, not during an outage. And where a model runs — what jurisdiction and whose infrastructure — is part of evaluating the platform, not a footnote to it.

The stack earns its keep twice. Availability is the obvious reason to have a second option on deck; the less obvious one is that an unrelated model is a diagnostic instrument — the thing you hand an answer you don't understand, to see whether an independent training base describes it the same way. That only works if it's genuinely unrelated, which is a good reason to keep the tiers on different providers rather than different sizes of the same one. Covered in Discernment.

Try it — the failover drill

Go 24 hours without your primary assistant. Not as a purity exercise — as a failover test, the same way you'd test a backup restore rather than assuming the backup works.

Two things to watch for, and the second is the valuable one:

  • Can you actually still work in your secondary? If the answer is "eventually, after fighting it for an hour," that's a cold standby, and you found out on a day it didn't matter.
  • Where does the itch actually show up? It won't be routine tasks and it won't be quick dispatch. It'll be the "something weird just broke and I need something that holds the thread while I think" moments. That's your real dependency, and knowing precisely where it is beats a vague sense that you use it a lot.

There's a side effect worth naming: without a model to hand a half-formed thought to, you have to finish forming it yourself first. That pre-thinking is exactly what separates a pull-prompt from a vending-machine request — which means the drill sharpens the skill it's testing.
