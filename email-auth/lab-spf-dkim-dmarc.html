Three separate mechanisms that get lumped together because they usually get configured together — but they check different things, and they break in different ways.

1. SPF: a hard ceiling nobody notices until it's crossed

An SPF record is just a TXT record listing who's allowed to send as your domain:

v=spf1 include:_spf.google.com include:sendgrid.net include:mailchimp.com ~all

Each include: is a DNS lookup — and each of those can trigger more lookups if the included record itself has includes. RFC 7208 caps the total at 10 DNS lookups, full stop. Cross it, and the record doesn't just ignore the overflow — the entire check returns PermError, which fails mail for every sender on the domain, not just whichever integration happened to push the count over ten.

This is a genuinely counterintuitive failure mode: the instinct is to blame whichever SaaS tool was added most recently, but the record fails as a whole regardless of which entry tipped it over. Removing the newest addition might drop the count back under ten and "fix" it — or it might not, if an earlier entry was actually the one pushing the total past the ceiling. Counting is the only way to know, not guessing from recency.

2. DKIM: breaks on migration, not on traffic

DKIM signs outgoing mail with a private key; the public half lives in a DNS TXT record at a predictable name: 

selector1._domainkey.example.com

Unlike SPF, DKIM rarely fails under normal operation — there's no lookup ceiling to cross. It breaks almost exclusively at two moments: a DNS provider migration where the selector record doesn't get recreated, or a routine key rotation on the sending provider's side that the DNS side never picks up. Both are "nothing changed from where you're sitting" failures — the mail server's configuration is identical, but the public key it's pointing to no longer matches.

3. DMARC: usually an alignment problem, not a broken record

DMARC doesn't check anything new by itself — it checks whether SPF and/or DKIM align with the domain in the visible From: header. A third-party sender (a marketing platform, a support ticketing tool) can pass SPF and DKIM cleanly for its own domain while the message's visible From: shows your domain — that's a DMARC alignment failure, and it's the far more common real-world case than the DMARC record itself being wrong.

Worth getting right, since it's easy to misquote: Google and Yahoo's 2024 bulk-sender authentication requirements (SPF + DKIM + DMARC, one-click unsubscribe) apply at 5,000 emails/day, not 500. A lower "500/day" figure, if you've heard it somewhere, is more likely a specific ESP or relay's own internal tier — not the well-known public rule everyone's actually citing.
