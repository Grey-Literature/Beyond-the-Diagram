Practice, not a shortcut

You learn its range by using it, its limits by pushing past them, and getting good takes practice, finesse, and enough domain experience to recognize when the thing in your hands is starting to lie to you.

This module treats the assistant as something you learn to wield, not a vending machine you feed symptoms into.

The instrument analogy

Same instrument.
Different possibilities.

01 / The technique

A guitar is not played one way.

You can strum it with your fingers or a pick. You can fingerpick it, tap it, use a glass or metal slide, run a violin bow across the strings, or — if you are sufficiently committed to the bit — play it with your teeth.

It is still the same instrument. But the technique changes the result dramatically.

AI works much the same way. A quick chat, deep research, a coding environment, connected files, a browser, persistent project context, or an autonomous agent may all involve the same underlying model. What changes is how you are playing it — what access you give it, how much context you provide, how tightly you guide it, and what kind of output you are trying to produce.

The skill is not simply knowing which instrument to pick.
It is knowing how to play it for the result you want.

02 / The instrument

Not every instrument sounds the same.

Technique is only part of the equation.

A Gibson does not sound or feel exactly like a Fender. Both are guitars. Both can play the same notes. Put them in the same musician's hands and ask for the same song, and the result can still be noticeably different.

AI models behave similarly.

Models that occupy roughly the same capability class are not interchangeable. They may differ in how they reason, how literally they follow direction, how readily they improvise, how consistently they behave across sessions, and how well they respond to particular kinds of work.

Then the way you play them changes the result again.

Chat with the model. Give it research tools. Put it in a coding environment. Connect it to files. Give it a browser. Let it operate as an agent.

Same instrument family. Different instrument.
Different technique. Different result.

Learning to wield AI means learning all three: what instrument you have, how it behaves, and how to play it for what you are trying to accomplish.

The meta-skill

The difference is
mostly the player.

It's a meta-skill, not a tenth domain.

Every technique here is gated on already knowing where the evidence lives, which is what the other nine modules are for — the assistant can help you find that evidence faster, or confidently point you at the wrong thing, and the difference is mostly the player.

Bring your own assistantNothing on these pages calls a model. The prompt comparisons are worked examples with the responses written out; the exercise is to run them yourself, against whatever assistant you actually use, and compare what comes back.
The framework

What getting good
at it takes.

Five skills. One continuing practice.

Choosing the instrument ↓
Whether this job wants an assistant at all, which one, and at what setting.
Knowing the instrument ↓
What every part of the product you use actually does, what it's good for in ITOps, and where each part runs out.
Working the partnership ↓
Briefing it, letting it interview you, feeding real evidence back each round — a conversation, not a query.
Checking the work ↓
The answer, the reasoning that produced it, and whether the session itself is still healthy.
Owning the result ↓
What you put in, what you disclose, and what you put your name to on the way out.

Running through all five is one question: who has hands on the keyboard? The assistant:

  1. Advisesyou decide and make the change
  2. Executesa task you defined exactly
  3. Actson a system, on its own judgment

The answer changes what you owe in checking and disclosure.

Where a tech starts, ITOps is almost entirely advisory — call it 90–95%, where a tech brings a problem to an assistant and the session turns out to be some mix of research, discovery, remediation and documentation (the ticket, the wiki, or both). That share doesn't stay put. It moves as the player grows with the instrument: one-off scripts turn into small reusable tools, and an agent starts showing up as a diagnostic instrument on a box you can afford to break. Executing and acting carry most of the risk whatever their share, which is why owning the result spends most of its time there — and why the share should grow on purpose rather than by drift.

Where this came from: the module started as notes on Anthropic's AI Fluency course, and some of its ideas trace back there. It has since been rebuilt around a different idea — the assistant as an instrument you practice with — and around ITOps specimens the course never covers, but the debt is real and worth stating.
